Part 1: Brush Fundamentals and Care

Brushes.
Put them down flat. Do not leave them in the rinse water jar resting on their bristles. The tip of the brush matters, do not wreck it, when you buy your own you will understand.

The Ferrule
Try not to fill the ferrule with paint. When you are doing washes or glazes it will wick in there, but that is ok, wash it out. Filling the back of the brush wastes the paint and will reduce the usefulness of the brush if it dries.

Size and Control
Use the largest brush that can get the job done. A larger brush will lead to less brush marks, less trips back to the palette. The tip of the brush and how you control your pressure, how you thin the paint is what will determine how finely you can paint, not the size of the hairs.

Maintenance Habits
Clean your brushes, when you finish with a colour, or change brushes. Adding water to your brush and wicking and shaping the point through-out your painting process is a good habit. Maintain the point.
Always try to get the thick paint out of the back of your brush. You can use water when the paint is wet but once it is dry you need brush soap. If it dries the hairs in front will separate.

Paint Management
Use less paint. It gives you more control.
Use more water and or medium. It gives you more control. These habits also save your brush somewhat.

Part 2:  Choosing Your Tools
Cost vs. Branding
My 4 brushes here are 300 dollars. This one is only 25 dollars, it is super nice. At some point you are paying for branding. If you look at what renaissance artists were using you will realise that you have great cheap tools.

Natural vs. Synthetic Fiber
Some nylon brushes will not wick paint in a good way. You will go back to the palette more. I like natural fibre. Natural hair from mice, sable etc, they are not pipes but tapered. Naturally shaped so liquid will drain down them away from the handle.
So my advice is, when you start, get 2 natural fibre brushes, cheap as you can find. Then when you 1. Know you want to do this for a long time and 2. Won’t ruin your brushes, then go and spend 5 to 25 per brush and get a couple of shapes you have experimented with.


Makeup Brushes
Cheap makeup brushes are good for a range of tasks, undercoating and dry brushing. In fact for dry brushing they are some of the best. We will demo dry brushing.

Part 3 Project 1: Base Coating the Organic Model
Setup
First lets start with an unprimed organic model. We use a flat acrylic through an airbrush to get the best result. You can also do it with a brush, but it is tough. Pick up the unpainted animal model. Put in front of you. Now choose a dark grey or dark brown.

The Wet Palette
Put some of that paint on your palette. Your wet palette is a thin sponge or a couple of layers of kitchen towel with baking paper on top, with water added. you want to keep it moist but not have water on top of the baking paper.

Application
Thin it a bit with a few drops of water and medium. Just a bit. Then pick up your largest brush and fill the front half of the bristles while mixing the paint into the water and medium. Now apply the paint to your horse or doggo or squig. All over.

Observations on Consistency
You will notice 3 things:
How much paint you have affects if you obscure details or leave brush marks.
How much medium you use affects how good the opacity of the paint is.
Water is helpful, but too much and the paint will bead. It will not stick.
If you use use too much paint it will be gluggy and you will see brush marks. If too much medium, multiple coats to get coverage. If too much water, multiple attempts to cover the surface and you will get rings when the paint dries. Watermarks, tidemarks. Ok. Now while that dries we pick up the dark figure.

Part 4: Mid-Session Brush Cleaning
Ok. Clean your brush. Now clean dip it in fresh water and paint the paper towel. Was it clean? Can you see colour? If so, not clean, clean again. Check the ferule, the base of the bristles. Can you see paint? Clean it out.

Part 5: Project 2: The Overbrushing Technique
Preparation
Acquire a black painted mini. If you are in the tutorial list there is one for you in front of you. Get your blunt fluffy brush, make sure it is dry and clean. Dab the end into your brown or dark grey. If you ran out or If it was super thin, start a new patch on your pallette.

The Process
Not a huge amount on your brush. Work your brush on the paper towel a bit to get the paint across the bristles. Now find a new bit of towel and wipe your brush from back to front until you have a mostly empty brush. When you run it over paper it should not flow but catch the raised parts. I call this heavy dry brush or over brush.
Now in circular motions get paint over the raised parts of the dark figure. You actually want to avoid the underneath. If there is a depression on either side of a belt, for example, you don't want to hit that depression. Go lightly. Hit the upper surfaces first while you have the most paint on your brush. It will look messy. I am going to say, trust the process.
Trust the process. Overbrushing is a technique. I use it sometimes. It will not win any competitions, but it can be a style on its own. Ok we have 4 more steps on this mini don’t panic.

Part 6: Project 2: Zenithal Dry Brushing
Setup
Ok. Clean your drybrush. In water, on towel. Give it a good soak, pull the paint out and dry it. Either make you brush bone dry or take another drybrush. Put a little bit of white on your pallette. Work it into the brush a bit. Clean it off on the towel. I test it on my palm, some folks use cardboard for this, so you can see light colours. Often you will be doing light dry brushing in light colours.
Technique
This time we are 100% doing dry brushing. No overbrush. You very much want little paint on the brush. You could use a dry retarder to help here. Dense, fine pigment and slow drying paints are best for this. Which is a problem because some whites have large pigment particles.
Ok now, from the top, zenithal, lightly brush the model. Where you hit an edge, hit it from 90 degrees. Go across it, not along. The aim is to add brightness to where light would fall.
Tip: Rotate the model in 3 dimensions. Keep your brush hand comfortable. Do not try to reach around. If you cannot see it, it doesn't need paint, no stress. Some people put the mini on a cork or paint pot as a handle.
So, from top, go lightly, less paint on brush, at 90 degrees to feature lines.

Part 7: Color Theory and Light Volume
Shadows and Highlights
Now generally we do not use pure white to highlight, nor pure black to do shadow. Reflected light on raised surfaces is rarely white in nature. And shadows are often not black. Shadows are deep blue-grey or dark brown, or a dark purplish. Many black paints have a blue or green base. Many white paints have a grey or blue tone.
Building Volume
But with this technique we are going apply colour pigment over the whole mini, and what we have done is build light volumes. Low volume in the crevasses, high volume on the points and raised areas. All paint is a bit translucent, and that can make life hard. If you try to paint an orangy yellow over green, you will be in for a hard time getting rid of the green without many layers, but we are going to exploit that to make our life easier.
Pigment Choice
Ok, but which pigments? Well because your minis are small, they do not reflect much light, so they need to be brighter and have more contrast than you expect. And because your mini has 3 dimensions, things meet. 2 colours will probably not be enough to define all the parts. It might be, but i would go for 3 main colours.
Break your expectations. Is skin tan or pink? What if it is brown? Or sickly brown green or pallid blue. If your model has skin or fur, that is one of your three colours. Here is a colour wheel. One of the reasons why the eye is drawn to some things is that they are adjacent on the mini to something opposite on the color wheel.
So pick 3 spots on the colour wheel. Write them down.

Part 8: Using Glazes and Contrast Paints
Mixing and Materials
You have 2 options here- either make a glaze or use an existing contrast paint.
We are going to take the glaze or contrast medium, some water and put some of just the first color you picked off the wheel. On the pallette, mix until it is pretty thin, it should pool a bit and where it is thin you should see thru it a bit. More medium than paint, just a bit of water.
Now, you can buy contrast paint already made up at 8 dollars a bottle or socalled speed paints at 6 dollars a bottle you have one in front of you. The odd bottle out. If it suits your scheme you should use it. Convenient but expensive. Tend to be fine pigments, strong colours. I have some favourites. Skeleton horde and snakebite leather from gamesworkshop are magic in a bottle. Actual game changers. Others are rubbish and will just get in your way.

Heavy Load Contrast Application
Ok we are going to do something counter intuitive that really only works with this method. We are going to load a large brush up. Not so it is dripping, but quite a bit of paint. You still don't want paint to flood into areas that don't need it. This is not a dip.
Then put the paint heavily over the whole area. It will make a defined edge, so for example on a cloak, you cannot do half and come back, you need to do it in one hit. Refill your brush when you start a new zone that is not continuous.

Suitability
This means it does not work well for flat surfaces like modern tanks, planes or gundam etc, at least in acrylic. It dries too fast and flat areas get uneven coverage. But for flowing cloth, detailed armour, fur, skin, wood it is amazing.

Cleanup
Ok, when you have covered your areas for that colour. Stop, quickly wet your brush, remove some off the towel, and go and remove any deep pooling before it starts to dry. Let it get coverage, but no blobs. Deep colour but not obscuring details.

Part 9: Refining Project 1: Texture and Transitions
Touch-ups
Now that will take a while to dry, so lets go back to our horse/doggo/squig. Do you need another coat? Less medium but still some, smidge of water, bit of paint, touch it up. Thinning out and coating widely will reduce brush strokes. Also paint along the features, so if there are brush strokes they enhance the model, not detract.

Texture and Fur
Ok. Now, concentrating on the fur/skin, what texture do you want? Take a tan or white and add it to your palette. Now take a bit of the base brown or dark grey and mix 2. Then thin with medium, and gently apply a thin layer to raised areas. Think about texture and flow of hair. You might want a series of strokes, or hatching you can see. Bear in mind that fur is in nature often darker at the ends, so you might not want to highlight the ends.

Blending
If you want a smooth transition across a surface, try adding a little tiny bit more white, some medium and while the most recent paint is still wet, blend in the new paint from the highest raised surfaces and the end points. This is easier with oils, and with acrylic you can use a drying retarder—a special medium so your paint dries slower.

Part 10: Environmental Lighting and Final Highlights
Environmental Tones
Think about the reflected light in the environment of your mini. If it is warm, add some yellow and or red, if it is cool add blue or green, not much. Then add a little more white or tan and then run along the edges of sharp areas or in the central points of curves or domes.

Edge Highlighting
Add even more white and a smidge of your light colour (warm yellow, cool blue) and remove the paint a little from your brush and make a sharp point. Add a dot to corners or the centre of curves. Maybe a super thin edge highlight. To do this use the side of your brush, not the tip, very lightly.
Note: Edge highlighting is very effective for armour. It can be used to define large panels, even “inside” the panel simply to draw attention to the limit of the panel as opposed to the outer rim. Logically this does not make sense, but when done consistently, it looks good. It mimics the contrast that the eye creates between 2 surfaces by lightening both surfaces, which enhances the dark line that would exist between them, even if such a line is not drawn.

Part 11: Finalizing the Contrast Model
While waiting for your layers and edges to dry, go back to your contrast model and apply another contrast colour of your 3 chosen colours. Fill the brush enough to get a complete section covered in one go. Apply a solid coat. Wash and empty your brush and clean up pooling.

Ok, now go back to the model with the layer and edge highlights. Take your base fur or skin colour and add a bit of black or brown to it. Now take the glaze medium. You want at least 50/50 medium, maybe even thinner, and maybe add 1/5th water.
